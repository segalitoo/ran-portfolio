Bingo Bay, art‑directed with AI

Take-home brief · AI Creative Manager role · 2026

A large casual games studio asked me to produce a whole Summer Festival event for a new 3D bingo game with AI, and to show the workflow behind it. I locked one art direction before the first render, then made 55 generations answer to it.

See all 55 frames ↓

What I was asked

Four parts: an AI production workflow, a finished creative package for a limited-time event, one step of that workflow automated, and the tools I would bring in next. The event and its world were mine to invent.

What I decided

Lock the look before generating anything. One render style, one light, eight colours, written down as tokens. Every output was then judged against the lock, not against how I felt about it that day.

What I made

Key art, two event screens, a background plate, a five-character cast, a music loop and store copy. Nano Banana Pro on Google Flow for images, Bria for cutouts, Suno for music, and my own hands for everything after.

01 · The lock

Golden Hour Bay, frozen before the first render.

Glossy, Pixar-like 3D, a low sunset with warm rim light, and eight colours. I wrote it down as tokens and assembled every prompt from them. Nothing was written freehand, so nothing could drift quietly.

The lock as a file

# summer-festival.dna.yml · v1.3
# owner: art director
STYLE    glossy stylized 3D render, Pixar-like
         casual mobile game asset
LIGHT    warm golden-hour lighting, subtle rim light
FORM     big expressive friendly eyes,
         soft rounded shapes
TECH     subsurface scattering, glossy highlights
PALETTE  FFC24B FF9E45 FF6F61 FF5C7A
         1FB8A6 F3D9A4 3A2A5C FFF4E2
# character = STYLE + [CHARACTER]
#   + FORM + TECH + LIGHT

Why a file, not a moodboard

A moodboard gets interpreted. A token gets pasted. When the art director changes one, the version goes up and every prompt built from it inherits the change.

Each asset records the version it was made with, so any image in the pack can be traced back to the exact style that produced it. The next event starts from this file, not from a blank prompt.

02 · The cast

Five characters, one sentence apart.

Four collectible beach pets with silhouettes you can tell apart at icon size, and a host who fronts the event without being a prize. Every character prompt is identical except for one bracketed slot. That is why 45 generations across five animals still read as a single collection.

The template

Cute collectible sea-creature character, glossy stylized 3D render, Pixar-like casual mobile game asset. [CHARACTER: a cheerful orange crab with raised claws and a tiny flower lei]. Big expressive friendly eyes, soft rounded shapes, glossy highlights, subsurface scattering, warm golden-hour lighting, subtle rim light. Centered, full body, clean neutral soft-gradient background for easy cutout, consistent character style, premium mobile game quality, high detail.

Judged as a set

Once the five keepers were picked, I put them side by side against the lock instead of approving them one at a time. Splash, Twinkle and Mango came back for a retouch to bring the light, the eye highlights and the cutout edges into line.

Twinkle took 12 rounds, the most of any character. Early versions were either a flat starfish with a face drawn on, or so detailed they broke the collection’s soft look.

03 · The pieces

Many candidates, one keeper, finished by hand.

Four visual pieces made from the approved keepers. The art is generated. The interface on top of it is design work, and that part is not done by prompting.

Background plate Base asset

Generated with no characters and no text, on purpose. It sits under the progression screen and under every popup after it, so it had to leave the middle calm enough for panels to land on. It was also the only background of ten rendered in the same 3D style as the cast.

04 · The soundtrack

Picked for the player who hears it forty times.

Five candidates from Suno on one brief: tropical pop, 70 BPM, ukulele, marimba, steel drum. The model gives you raw material. A loop a game can ship is a separate craft step.

Bingo at the Beach

The game-ready master, looped and limited by hand

The close call

My ear preferred Sunny Reception. The ukulele felt warmer, and I nearly led with it. So I listened again against the real brief: a loop that has to survive thirty repetitions on a phone speaker without stepping on the win fanfares.

On that test Bingo at the Beach stayed pleasant and Sunny Reception started to grate. I would happily hear the runner-up once. I picked for the player who hears it forty times a session.

Finished by hand

The raw render was not loop-ready. I cut the soft intro that dipped on every repeat, built the loop from the 35 to 117 second section with a two-second equal-power crossfade back into the head, and pulled the true peak from a clipping 0.0 down to −1 dBTP.

The crossfade hides a slightly imperfect beat alignment at the seam. With more time I would re-prompt for a shorter stem with a set bar count rather than keep patching a 2.6-minute render.

07 · The process

Nine stages, and a person at every gate.

The workflow this event ran through, written so it can run again. AI does the volume at every stage. The person there works as an editor, not an operator, and choosing the one of twelve that ships is the part that matters.

Where it jams

  1. 01

    The review queue. Automated pre-checks and batch approvals, so people only see what passed.

  2. 02

    Consistency at volume. Locked references, seeds and template prompts.

  3. 03

    Rights and compliance. Pre-cleared kits, handled by market priority.

  4. 04

08 · What I’d bring next

Three tools, each against a specific pain.

Not the obvious picks. Legal exposure on generated art, static UI in a live game, and the gap between a 2D concept and a 3D model. Two of the three I ran on Mango, the event’s own host.

→

Bria

Image generation trained on licensed data

Tested
I re-posed the approved Mango through my own tool on the Bria API. Same character, a new pose, no redraw.
Why
For a public company the question is less which model looks best and more which output legal can approve. Indemnified generation removes that blocker.
Limit
Its raw aesthetic trails the frontier models, so it is the production and editing layer, with exploration done elsewhere.

Rive

Interactive animation that runs in the game

Try it
A CSS stand-in for the kind of button Rive ships as a tiny file. It idles, and it reacts when you hover or press it.
Why
Casual games live on juicy UI. A state machine in a few kilobytes lets the lobby breathe, and a designer can edit it without an engineer.
Limit
Vector first, so the painterly 3D renders still come in as raster layers.
→

Meshy

Image to 3D, for a game that is 3D

Tested
I fed the approved 2D Mango into Meshy 6 and had a recognisable 3D base mesh in minutes, lei and pose intact.
Why
In a 3D game the expensive part is not the concept, it is the model. Artists start from a draft instead of an empty viewport.
Limit
A blockout, never a shippable asset. Topology and rigging still need a professional pass.
